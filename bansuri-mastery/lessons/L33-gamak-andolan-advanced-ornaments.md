## 1. Learning objectives

1. Explain shruti (microtones) and why some ragas need intonation a tuner cannot fully describe (PIT-06).
2. Play an andolan (slow oscillation) on the correct note in a raga context (ORN-05).
3. Play gamak using breath pulses and finger movement, with each note clear (ORN-06).
4. Demonstrate one zamzama pattern (ORN-07, optional).
5. Use kampan/vibrato deliberately and explain your choice (ORN-08).
6. Play one phrase in two deliberately different tone colours (TON-09).
7. Name ornaments heard in a recording (EAR-13).

## 2. Prerequisites

Clean meend and kan; all komal notes in tune ([L25](L25-komal-swaras-and-12-notes.md));
breath attacks ("hu/ha") without the tongue ([L13](L13-articulation-tonguing-and-slurs.md));
4 notes per beat at 70+ BPM.

## 3. Simple explanation

### Shruti (PIT-06)

Indian music theory describes **22 shrutis** (microtonal positions) in an octave, of which the
12 notes you know are a selection. **Fact:** in practice, performers place some notes slightly
differently in different ragas. Example often cited: komal Re in Bhairav is played lower (and
often oscillated) compared with komal Re in some other ragas.

> **Disputed:** scholars disagree about the exact sizes of the 22 shrutis and whether modern
> performance follows them precisely. What is agreed: **raga intonation is learned by ear from
> recordings and teachers**, and a tuner reading alone is not enough.

### Andolan (ORN-05)

A **slow, gentle swing** of a note, reaching slightly toward a neighbouring pitch and back,
without clearly sounding the neighbour. Examples commonly cited: komal Re and komal Dha in
Bhairav; komal Ga and komal Dha in Darbari Kanada (a raga you will study later).

On bansuri, andolan is made by **slowly sliding a finger partly over and off a hole** (and/or
small changes of the blowing angle), in a slow regular wave.

### Gamak (ORN-06)

A **forceful oscillation** between notes, giving weight and energy. On bansuri there are two
main ways:

| Type | How | Sound |
|---|---|---|
| Breath gamak | Pulses of air from the diaphragm ("ha-ha-ha" with no tongue), often on each note of a taan | Each note "pushed", like a vocalist's gamak taan |
| Finger gamak | Rapid movement between the main note and the upper neighbour, with the main note emphasised | A shaking, heavy ornament |

### Kampan / vibrato (ORN-08, TON-09)

**Kampan** is a small, regular wavering of pitch or volume. **Disputed:** bansuri players differ
greatly. Some use breath vibrato often (especially in light music); many classical teachers
prefer a straight tone on long notes and reserve wavering for specific raga contexts. This
curriculum's **Recommendation**: learn to play perfectly straight long notes first; add
vibrato only as a deliberate choice, never as a habit.

### Zamzama (ORN-07, Enrich)

Fast clusters of repeated neighbour notes, a virtuoso decoration. Optional.

## 4. Step-by-step instructions

### Step 1. Andolan (2-3 weeks)

1. Drone on C. Play komal Re (`r`, `XXH OOO`) and hold it in tune for 6 s.
2. Now very slowly cover a little *more* of hole 3, so the pitch sinks slightly (just a few
   cents), then return. One full wave should take about 1-2 seconds.
3. Keep the waves **slow, even and narrow**. The listener should still hear `r`, not two notes.
4. Practise in the Bhairav phrase `G m r^ - S` (andolan on komal Re). Raga card:
   [Bhairav](../ragas/bhairav.md).
5. Compare with a recording. Adjust width and speed to match.

**What you should hear:** a "breathing", slowly swaying note. If it sounds like a trill or
wobble, it is too fast or too wide.

### Step 2. Breath gamak (3-4 weeks)

1. On Sa, play a long note, then add soft pulses: "haa-a-haa-a-haa". The note never stops; only
   its intensity pulses.
2. Make the pulses regular with a metronome: 1 pulse per beat at 60 BPM, then 2, then 4.
3. Apply one pulse per note on a slow scale: `S R G P D S'` (Bhupali), each note pulsed once.
4. Increase speed to a taan: `SR GP DS' DP | GR S-` with every note pulsed.

**What you should hear:** every note clearly separated by a push of air, without any tongue
"t" sound and without the tone cracking.

**Safety:** breath gamak uses strong diaphragm work. Stop if you feel dizzy
([TS-07](../troubleshooting/TS-07-breath-dizziness-and-endurance.md)).

### Step 3. Finger gamak (3-4 weeks)

1. Choose `G`. Its upper neighbour in most ragas is `m` or `M`; in Bhupali it is `P`.
2. Alternate `G` and the neighbour **slowly**, with `G` longer and stronger: `G-m G-m G-m`.
3. Speed up gradually while keeping `G` dominant. The neighbour becomes a fast "flick".
4. Combine with breath pulses on each `G`.

### Step 4. Kampan and tone colour (TON-09, ORN-08)

1. Play a phrase (for example `G R S .N S`) with a **completely straight** tone. Record.
2. Play it with a gentle, slow breath vibrato only on the last long `S`. Record.
3. Tone colour: play the phrase **dark** (lips slightly more covering the hole, slower, warmer
   air) and **bright** (slightly less covered, faster air). Keep the pitch steady with the tuner.
4. Ask a listener which was which. Write a sentence about which version suits which context.

### Step 5. Zamzama (optional, Phase 5)

Example around `G`: `GmGR GmGR G-` at a slow tempo, then faster. The notes are clean,
repeated, and light.

### Step 6. Ornament ear training (EAR-13)

Take a 3-minute bansuri recording. Every time you hear an ornament, write the timestamp and the
type (kan, meend, murki, khatka, andolan, gamak, vibrato). Do this for 10 marked moments, then
check with a teacher, or with the descriptions in [R-W02](../02-RESOURCE-LIBRARY.md#2-written-lessons-and-fingering-charts).

## 8. Independent exercises

1. Record a Bhairav phrase with and without andolan. Compare with a master recording.
2. Play an 8-note Bhupali taan with breath gamak at medium speed.
3. Play one phrase in two tone colours and explain the choice in writing.

## 9. Common mistakes and corrections

| Mistake | Correction |
|---|---|
| Andolan too fast (sounds like vibrato) | Slow it to 1-2 s per wave; use a metronome |
| Andolan on the wrong note | Check the raga card; andolan is raga-specific |
| Gamak notes blur | Slow down; one clear pulse per note |
| Tone cracks during breath gamak | Smaller pulses; keep the lip opening stable ([TS-02](../troubleshooting/TS-02-squeaks-and-wrong-octave.md)) |
| Vibrato on every long note | Practise straight tone first; use vibrato only by choice |
| Pitch drifts during tone-colour changes | Tuner on; adjust angle and air together ([TS-05](../troubleshooting/TS-05-pitch-out-of-tune.md)) |

## 10. Quiz

1. How is andolan different from vibrato?
2. Name the two main ways to make gamak on bansuri.
3. Why is a tuner not enough for raga intonation?
4. Name one raga where andolan is commonly taught, and on which note.

<details markdown="1"><summary>Answer key</summary>

1. Andolan is slower, raga-specific, and swings toward a neighbouring pitch; vibrato is faster, narrower and a general expressive device.
2. Breath (diaphragm pulses) and finger movement (alternating with a neighbour note).
3. Some ragas place notes slightly differently (shruti) and use moving notes; this is learned by ear.
4. Bhairav (komal Re and komal Dha) or Darbari Kanada (komal Ga and komal Dha).

</details>
