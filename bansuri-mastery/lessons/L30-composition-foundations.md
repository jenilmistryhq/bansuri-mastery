## 1. Learning objectives

1. Write, notate and play an original 16-beat melody (CMP-01).
2. Use question-answer phrasing, repetition with variation and contrast to make a 16-bar melody memorable (CMP-02).
3. Compose a sthayi and antara in a known raga and tala (CMP-03).
4. Set a short text or syllable pattern to melody, respecting word stress (CMP-04).

## 2. Prerequisites

You can improvise short phrases (L29) and write rhythmic sargam accurately (L10). Part B needs
one raga you know well.

## 3. Simple explanation

Composition is improvisation that you **keep**: you choose the best ideas, write them down
and polish them. Unlike improvisation, you can take your time.

Most memorable melodies use a small number of tools:

| Tool | What it means | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| Question and answer | Phrase 1 ends "open", phrase 2 ends "closed" (on Sa) | Like a sentence and its full stop |
| Repetition | Repeat an idea | Listeners remember it |
| Variation | Repeat it with one change | Interest without confusion |
| Contrast | A new section in a different register or rhythm | Keeps attention (this is what an antara does) |
| A clear goal | The melody heads somewhere (a high point, then home) | Gives shape |

## 4. Step-by-step instructions

### Part A: Short melodies (CMP-01, CMP-02)

**Step 1. Choose limits.** Notes: `.D S R G P D` (pentatonic). Tala: keherwa (8 beats). Length:
2 cycles = 16 beats. Tempo: 70 BPM.

**Step 2. Write a rhythm first.** Clap it, then write it with dummy notes:

```
X X X X | X - X - ||  X X X X | X X X - ||
```

**Step 3. Write the question (8 beats) ending on an open note (R, G, P or D):**

```
S R G P | G - R - ||
X         0
```

**Step 4. Write the answer (8 beats) ending on Sa.** Pick up near where the question stopped,
climb a little higher for interest, then come home:

```
G P D P | G R S - ||
X         0
```

The complete 16-beat melody:

```
S R G P | G - R - || G P D P | G R S - ||
X         0          X         0
```

**Step 5. Play it from your notation exactly.** If what you play differs from what you wrote,
fix the notation, not your memory. Record it (CMP-01).

**Step 6. Build 16 bars (CMP-02).** Use the classic plan **A A' B A'**, each A being 8 beats
(one keherwa cycle):

| Section | Content | Ends on |
|---|---|---|
| A | Main idea (question) | R or G |
| A' | Same idea, new ending (answer) | S |
| B | Contrast: higher register, different rhythm | P or D |
| A' | Return of the answer | S |

Example skeleton (pentatonic, 4 x 8 beats):

```
A :  S R G P | G - R - ||
A':  S R G P | G R S - ||
B :  P D S' - | D P G P ||
A':  S R G P | G R S - ||
```

Label each section on your score. Then improve it: add a rest, change one rhythm, add one kan.

### Part B: Raga composition (CMP-03)

**Step 7. Learn the parts of a bandish** (composition):

- **Sthayi**: the first part; mostly in mandra and madhya, centred around Sa. Its opening
  phrase is the **mukhda**, which returns again and again in performance.
- **Antara**: the second part; rises to the upper madhya and taar saptak, then returns to the
  sthayi.

**Step 8. Use raga material.** Choose Bhupali or Yaman. Write down: allowed notes, aroh,
avroh, pakad and nyas notes from the [raga card](../ragas/README.md). Your melody must obey
them.

**Step 9. Compose the sthayi (16 beats, teentaal).** Start with a mukhda that contains the
pakad or another characteristic phrase. Land on sam with an important note (often Sa or the
vadi).

**Step 10. Compose the antara.** Begin around Pa or Ga, rise to taar Sa, touch `R'` or `G'`,
and come back to end in a way that leads into the mukhda.

**Step 11. Check** against the raga rules checklist in [L31](L31-alap-and-swar-vistar.md#raga-rules-checklist).
Ask: would a listener recognise the raga within the first cycle?

**Step 12. Play with a tabla loop**, sthayi twice, antara twice, sthayi once.

### Part C: Melody for words (CMP-04)

**Step 13.** Choose a short poem (your own, or a traditional text out of copyright). Split it
into syllables and mark the **stressed** syllables (the ones you naturally say louder or
longer).

**Step 14.** Put stressed syllables on strong beats (sam and tali beats) and on longer or
higher notes. Put unstressed syllables on weak beats and shorter notes.

**Step 15.** Write notes with the syllables underneath:

```
Notes:      S  R  G  -  | P  G  R  -
Syllables:  ma ne ki ba | ta su no -
Beats:      1  2  3  4  | 5  6  7  8
```

(The syllables above are placeholders: replace them with your text.) Sing it; if a word sounds
unnatural, move it to a different beat.

## 5. Diagram: shape of a good melody

```
 pitch
  ^                 * high point (B section)
  |              *     *
  |      *    *          *
  |   *     *              *
  | *                         * home (Sa)
  +--------------------------------> time
    question  answer  contrast  return
```

## 8. Independent exercises

From [EX-IMP](../exercises/EX-IMP-improv-and-composition-prompts.md), do the composition prompts:
for example "a melody using only `S R G` that sounds happy", "a melody whose high point is
`S'`", "a lullaby in Dadra".

## 9. Common mistakes and corrections

| Mistake | Correction |
|---|---|
| No clear ending | End the last phrase on Sa, on a strong beat, with a longer note |
| Too many ideas | Use one main idea and vary it |
| Rhythm the same everywhere | Give the B section a different rhythm |
| Raga composition breaks raga rules | Check every phrase against the raga card and checklist |
| Notation and playing disagree | Treat the notation as the contract; fix one or the other |
| Stressed syllables on weak beats | Move them to sam/tali beats or longer notes |

## 10. Performance test

1. Play your 16-beat melody (CMP-01) from your own notation at 70 BPM; record it.
2. Present your A A' B A' melody with sections labelled (CMP-02).
3. (Phase 4) Perform your bandish with a tabla loop: sthayi, antara, sthayi (CMP-03).
4. (Phase 4) Sing and play your 8-line song melody with syllables written (CMP-04).

<details markdown="1"><summary>What a successful composition shows</summary>

- The melody is playable exactly as written.
- A listener can hum the main idea after hearing it twice.
- The ending feels finished (on Sa).
- In a raga composition, the raga is recognisable from the mukhda.
- In a song, words fall naturally on the beats.

</details>

## 11. Mastery criteria
